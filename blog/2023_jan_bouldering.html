Starting My Bouldering Journey

In January 2023, after a few years of not being physically active post-Covid, I decided to take on a new challenge and hobby: bouldering. A couple of things held me back at first—physically, I was weak and definitely out of shape, but more than that, I’ve always been deathly afraid of heights. However, I felt like it was time to push myself beyond those fears.

I got a membership at Crux, a local bouldering gym in Austin. They currently have central and south locations, with a new one coming soon in north Austin. Crux’s grading system associates the V-scale with animals, which I thought was a fun and memorable way to represent difficulty:

Crux South location

  • V0 and VB are represented by pandas and sloths—beginner routes that almost anyone can attempt.
  • V1-V2 are the "koalas"—a good starting point for many first-time climbers.
  • V3-V4 are the "raccoons"—where the need for real technique and training begins.
  • V5-V6 are the "squirrels"—these grades seemed intimidating the first time I saw people climbing them.
  • V7-V8 are the "goats," followed by V9-V10 ("bears") and V11+ ("monkeys"). For me, anything V7 and beyond felt impossible.

V7-V8 rated goat tag

Starting out, I realized just how bad I was at climbing. The highest grade I could climb was either a V1 or maybe a V2 on a good day. My arm stamina was also really low, limiting me to about an hour of climbing before I couldn’t hold on to anything. To add to that, I had to face my fear of heights. Letting go and trusting a fall from 5, or sometimes 10 feet, took some getting used to.

Still, I was determined to learn on my own in the beginning. No watching videos, no asking for advice—I wanted to see how far I could push myself without external help. My goal? To climb a V7 ("goat") by the end of the year. After a couple of weeks, I managed to break into V3-V4 ("raccoon") climbs, but it was clear that the more advanced routes would require long-term training and dedication.

One of the things that hooked me was how every boulder problem is completely new. You have to figure out your own solution—there are very few restrictions on how you approach the climb, and it’s up to you to discover the best way to conquer each problem. This constant change made bouldering exciting and fresh every time. I especially enjoyed dynamic moves, which reminded me of the quick, agile movements from volleyball, another sport I love.

Despite the challenges, I found myself really enjoying bouldering. It was a fun puzzle, physically demanding, and always different—everything I was looking for in a sport.
